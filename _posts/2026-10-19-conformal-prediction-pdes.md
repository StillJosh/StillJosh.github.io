title: "Conformal prediction for time-dependent PDEs"
description: "Why non-exchangeable conformal prediction fails on function spaces, and when it works on discretized linear PDEs."
---

Conformal prediction (CP) is a neat method to estimate the uncertainty of a model. Its simplicity and provable guarantees made it one of the hottest topics in ML over the past years. With dozens of papers in top-rated venues, it has seemingly been applied in every way possible, extending even into areas where it is not (naturally) possible. One such area is time series.

In time series, we often have to assume that the data distribution may change arbitrarily over time and that a deployed model at some point might produce arbitrarily bad predictions, not hedgeable by anything we know from the training time. While this contradicts the prerequisites of (classic) Conformal Prediction, there are multiple extensions that either relax its implications, e.g., by guaranteeing coverage only on average over an infinite time frame (see Adaptive Conformal Prediction, [Gibbs & Candès, 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/0d441de75945e5acbc865406fc9a2559-Paper.pdf), and related work), or by increasing the prerequisites, e.g., through knowledge about the time series ([Oliveira et al., 2024](http://jmlr.org/papers/v25/23-1553.html)), the error of the model ([Auer et al., 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/aef75887979ae1287b5deb54a1e3cbda-Paper-Conference.pdf)), or the distributional change ([Barber et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1214/23-AOS2276)).

Personally, I do not see CP being applied in life-or-death situations where minor deviations from the coverage guarantees could be critical. Therefore, due to its simplicity, I find Adaptive Conformal Prediction and its variants to be the most reasonable choice for time series applications. Nonetheless, the problem sparked our curiosity. Our field of study, partial differential equations (PDEs), which describe physical processes, is full of time series with additional structure, and there exists a plethora of mathematical tools to analyze their behavior. This raises the question whether we can leverage these properties to enable CP on PDEs.

For this article, it suffices to view PDEs as a time series on a continuous domain, e.g. functions $$u(x,t): [0,1] \times [0, \infty) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$$ that have values at a point in space (here, a location on the interval from $$0$$ to $$1$$) and time (here, any time from $$0$$ to $$\infty$$). Often enough, these functions are discretized in space and time, e.g., only taking values on a grid $$\left\{ 0, \frac{1}{10}, \frac{2}{10}, \dots, 1 \right\}$$ and at time points $$\left\{ 0, 1, 2, \dots \right\}$$. We will then write $$\mathbf{u}^t:=\left( u(0,t), u(\frac{1}{10},t), u(\frac{2}{10},t), \dots, u(1,t) \right)$$. This dichotomy raised one of our initial questions: Are there any differences between CP on the functional and the discrete form?

We will visualize some results using the heat equation. The heat equation is one of the simplest PDEs and describes how heat spreads on an object over time. Essentially, every point gets either hotter or colder, depending on whether its surrounding area is hotter or colder than itself. The visualization below shows how the time series evolves for the functional and the discrete form, depending on the initial temperature distribution.

Usually, we observe the temperature of the object at some time point $$t$$ and would like to predict it for some future time point $$t+1$$. Additionally, we would like to know how certain the model is in its prediction. This (un-)certainty is usually defined by a probability distribution over the predicted values, propagating from some probability distribution on the input data through the model.

Now, in Conformal Prediction, we usually train our model and then estimate the error distribution on a held-out calibration set. We can then add the value of a bad-enough error (e.g., the 90% quantile of the errors) on top of our prediction and construct *prediction bands*. If the test data follows the same distribution as the calibration data, we even get probabilistic guarantees that the true test value will be within the prediction bands.

Unfortunately, in time series, when we calibrate at a time $$T$$ and test at a time $$t>T$$, the data distribution may have shifted, and with it the error distribution of our model. Thus, our probabilistic guarantees vanish and we cannot know if our prediction bands are good or not. Non-exchangeable CP ([Barber et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1214/23-AOS2276)) aims at pricing this shift into our prediction bands, recovering the probabilistic guarantees. By estimating how far the distributions shifted, measured by the total variation distance (TV distance), and adding twice that quantity to the error quantile we pick, we recover the coverage guarantee. So if we wanted the test sample to be covered by our prediction bands with 90% chance and the calibration and test distributions have a 0.02 TV distance, we take the 94% quantile of the calibration errors. If the TV distance gets too large, the resulting prediction bands will be uninformative. The animation below shows the TV distance for two Gaussian distributions and how we have to adjust our prediction bands depending on their shift.

As a first step in the paper, we want to investigate the TV distance in a function space setting to find out if we can apply non-exchangeable CP there. For this, we take the simple example of the heat equation again and assume the initial temperature distribution along a 1D line segment is Gaussian (in particular, we assume a Gaussian distribution on a function space, which is a bit more complicated than "normal" Gaussian distributions. For this demonstration it suffices to imagine that we randomly pick a somewhat smooth function fluctuating around 0). Our theorem shows that for any evolution time $$t>0$$, no matter how small, the TV distance between the initial and the final temperature distribution is always $$1$$. This implies that even if our calibration and test time points are just an $$\varepsilon$$ apart, we cannot apply (non-exchangeable) CP on them. The intuition behind this is that both distributions are distributions on an infinite-dimensional (function) space. Thus, tiny changes accumulate over the infinite dimensions to result in mutually singular distributions. While we show this only on one example with the heat equation, this is a common behavior of distributions on function spaces.

While this result undermines the success of CP on functional data, it has little effect in practice. There, we usually work with a discretized version of a function, i.e., a finite-dimensional vector. And indeed, starting with an initial temperature vector from a finite-dimensional Gaussian, the TV distance between any two time points will always be strictly smaller than $$1$$. Further, if we take a small enough time step, our CP prediction bands will even be bounded, resulting in usable uncertainty quantification.

In the paper, we provide a more general proposition, showing that we can get CP bands from any linear PDE with an initial distribution from a family that is closed under affine transformations (e.g., Gaussian, Cauchy, Laplace, and more). We use the algorithm from [Tibshirani et al. (2019)](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/8fb21ee7a2207526da55a679f0332de2-Paper.pdf), which constructs the CP bands from the likelihood ratio between test and calibration densities. If the likelihood ratio of a test sample exceeds a certain threshold, prediction bands become infinitely wide and coverage is trivial. In general, this preserves the target coverage of the test samples over time, until all test samples get infinite bands and coverage of the test samples is 100% (but also all bands are trivial). In practice, we would estimate the mean and covariance, needed for the likelihood ratio, from the data.

Seeing how the infinite-dimensional function space always yielded a TV distance of $$1$$, but the finite-dimensional setting could give reasonable bands, we further investigated the reason for this behavior. Indeed, it is not dimensionality itself that is driving this issue. For a simple drifting time series that shifts in mean with a constant pace, we show that the drift direction relative to the covariance of the initial distribution is the deciding factor. As this is implicitly also affected by the dimensionality of our problem, we can mitigate the CP degeneration by restricting our prediction to a subset of our dimensions. To be precise, the prediction of our model can still use the full data space as input, but we need to select the dimensions for which we want to get prediction bands (our paper shows some examples).

<figcaption>A drift in two dimensions $(u_1, u_2)$ with correlated Gaussian initial distribution. Left: calibration (green) and test (orange) distribution. Drag the test distribution or use the sliders. Ellipses mark one and two standard deviations. Right: TV distance when we want bands for both coordinates and for $u_1$ only. A drift along the wide axis of the covariance costs little. A drift along the narrow axis costs much. Restricting to $u_1$ never increases the TV distance.</figcaption>
</figure>

In conclusion, we have shown that on a function space, non-exchangeable CP will usually fail, while in discrete space, we can get robust prediction bands for linear PDEs by simply estimating the mean and covariance of the data. However, in that restricted setting, there are usually more straightforward methods to achieve accurate predictions and uncertainty bands than using AI models and CP. Our contribution should be viewed as a theoretical one and we hope to inspire future work investigating different properties of PDEs and certain subclasses of PDEs that could unlock non-exchangeable CP in that setting.
